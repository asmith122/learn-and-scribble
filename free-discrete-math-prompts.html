How to use these prompts

Copy the instruction block below and paste it before any prompt in this pack. It makes the turn-by-turn behavior more reliable.

Start with this instruction block

Use this for practice permitted by my course policy. Ask one question at a time and wait for my response. You may give worked demonstrations when the selected prompt requests them. For problems assigned to me to solve, do not provide a complete solution before I have made a genuine attempt, and follow any stricter no-solution instructions in the selected prompt. Accept alternative valid methods. If my statement is false, ambiguous, or missing assumptions, help me identify the problem rather than forcing a proof. If you are uncertain, say so instead of marking my work correct. At the end, summarize one strength and one thing to review.

Use these prompts to learn

Use these prompts to learn, never to outsource graded work. Each prompt asks the assistant to explain a concept, quiz you, generate fresh practice problems, or check reasoning you wrote yourself. None of them asks for a finished answer to hand in. If your instructor's course policy limits AI use, that policy controls: use the prompts for studying and self-testing, and ask your instructor before using a prompt in connection with graded work.

Free sample 1 of 33

Prompt 1: Explain implication and its relatives

Topic: Propositional logic. Best for: Starting a topic, or untangling converse errors before an exam.

Act as my discrete math tutor. Explain the difference between an implication p -> q, its converse, its inverse, and its contrapositive, using one everyday example (not a math example). Then give me three new statements and ask me to write each one's converse and contrapositive myself. Wait for my answers, correct them one at a time, and explain any mistake instead of just giving the right version.

Prompt 6: Check my proof, line by line

Topic: Proof techniques. Best for: Getting honest feedback on a proof you drafted yourself.

Act as my discrete math tutor and review a proof I wrote myself, pasted in brackets: [Paste the original problem, relevant domains and assumptions, any required method, and your own attempt.] Read it line by line. Tell me the first line where the logic breaks, a definition is misused, or a step is asserted without justification. Name the problem in plain language, give me a hint, and stop so I can revise that line myself. Do not rewrite my proof. When you find no further errors, identify any explanation that remains unclear.

Free sample 8 of 33

Prompt 8: Practice problems: subsets, power sets, and identities

Topic: Sets. Best for: Drill you can repeat until set notation feels automatic.

Act as my discrete math tutor and give me practice, one problem at a time. Mix three kinds of problems: listing the subsets or power set of a small set, simplifying an expression with set identities, and deciding whether a set equality is true or false. When asking whether a proposed set identity holds for all sets, require a proof if I say it is true or a counterexample if I say it is false. Start easy and get harder as I answer correctly. Do not show the solution before I answer. When I miss one, explain the idea I need, then give me a similar problem so I can try the idea again.

Free sample 12 of 33

Prompt 12: Check my injectivity or surjectivity proof

Topic: Functions. Best for: Feedback on the two most common function proofs in the course.

Act as my discrete math tutor and check a proof I wrote myself, pasted in brackets: [Paste the original problem, relevant domains and assumptions, any required method, and your own attempt.] Check whether my proof uses a valid strategy. For a direct injectivity proof, I may start with arbitrary a and b in the domain and assume f(a) = f(b); a valid contrapositive proof is also acceptable. For surjectivity, check that every element of the stated codomain is covered and that any proposed preimage belongs to the domain. Then find the first step that does not follow. Give me a hint rather than the fix, and wait for my revision. Do not write the proof for me.

Free sample 17 of 33

Prompt 17: Practice choosing a counting approach before calculating

Topic: Combinatorics and counting. Best for: Breaking the habit of reaching for a formula first.

Act as my discrete math tutor. Give me one counting or pigeonhole problem at a time, and for each one, ask me first whether order matters, whether repetition is allowed, whether the objects are distinct, and whether there are restrictions. Allow "not applicable" when one of these modeling questions does not fit the problem, and ask me to explain why. Then ask me to propose a counting model and justify it. Accept any valid method, including a combination of methods. Do not let me calculate or begin the proof until I have justified my approach, and if my model is wrong, ask me a question about the problem that exposes why. Across the five problems, include one problem each that rewards a case split, complementary counting, inclusion-exclusion, stars and bars, and a pigeonhole argument. Run five problems, increasing in difficulty, then summarize the clues I kept missing.

Free sample 26 of 33

Prompt 26: Coach my induction proof, step by step

Topic: Induction and recursion. Best for: Writing your own induction proof with a coach, not a ghostwriter.

Act as my discrete math tutor and coach me through writing an induction proof of a statement I paste in brackets: [paste the statement]. Work in stages and do not draft any stage for me. First, ask me to write the base case and confirm it. Next, ask me to state the inductive hypothesis in my own words, exactly as I will use it. Then ask me to attempt the inductive step, and only hint when I stall, one hint at a time. Finish by asking me to read the whole proof aloud, in order, and explain why I believe each step is justified. If a gap remains, ask me one question that helps me address it. Otherwise, ask me to explain why the inductive step applies to every integer in the stated range.

Free sample 31 of 33

Prompt 31: Explain why a tree on n vertices has n - 1 edges

Topic: Trees. Best for: Understanding a classic result instead of memorizing it.

Act as my discrete math tutor. Explain what makes a finite undirected graph a tree, then help me establish that a tree with n ≥ 1 vertices has exactly n − 1 edges. Start with small trees I can draw, have me count vertices and edges for n = 1, 2, 3, and 4, and ask me what pattern I see. Make clear that the small examples suggest the result but do not prove it. Guide me to justify that every finite tree with at least two vertices has a leaf, and that deleting a leaf and its incident edge leaves a tree. Use that fact to help me build a complete inductive argument. Then ask me to state the full explanation back to you in my own words and correct anything I leave out.
